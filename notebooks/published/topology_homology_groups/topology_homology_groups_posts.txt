Ever wonder how mathematicians rigorously define what a "hole" is?

Enter homology theory. We break shapes into triangles, track their boundaries with matrices, and compute quotient groups.

The result: Betti numbers that count holes by dimension.

B0 = connected components
B1 = 1D holes (loops)

Implemented simplicial homology computation in Python today.

Key insight: Chain complexes C_n with boundary operators d_n satisfy d_n . d_{n+1} = 0, meaning im(d_{n+1}) is in ker(d_n).

Homology H_n = ker(d_n) / im(d_{n+1}) measures cycles that aren't boundaries.

Computed Betti numbers for:
- S1: B0=1, B1=1
- S2: B0=1, B1=0, B2=1
- T2: B0=1, B1=2, B2=1
- Klein bottle: H1 = Z + Z/2Z (torsion!)
- RP2: H1 = Z/2Z

Smith Normal Form makes this tractable.

Title: I built a homology calculator in Python - here's how algebraic topology counts "holes" in shapes

Body:

**What is homology?**

Homology theory is a branch of algebraic topology that associates sequences of groups (called homology groups) to topological spaces. These groups encode information about "holes" of various dimensions.

**The key ideas:**

1. We represent a shape as a **simplicial complex** - basically triangles glued together
2. The **boundary operator** maps each triangle to its edges with alternating signs
3. The crucial property: taking the boundary twice always gives zero (d . d = 0)
4. **Homology** = cycles that aren't themselves boundaries

**Betti numbers tell us:**
- B0 = number of connected components
- B1 = number of 1-dimensional holes (loops/tunnels)
- B2 = number of 2-dimensional voids (cavities)

**Results from my implementation:**

| Space | H0 | H1 | H2 | Euler Char |
|-------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Circle S1 | Z | Z | 0 | 0 |
| Sphere S2 | Z | 0 | Z | 2 |
| Torus T2 | Z | Z+Z | Z | 0 |
| Klein bottle | Z | Z + Z/2Z | 0 | 0 |
| RP2 | Z | Z/2Z | 0 | 1 |

The torsion (Z/2Z terms) in non-orientable surfaces like the Klein bottle and projective plane is fascinating - it captures the "twisting" that happens when you can't consistently orient the surface.

**The algorithm:**

I used the Smith Normal Form to diagonalize the boundary matrices over the integers. This gives you both the rank (free part) and the torsion coefficients directly.

**ELI5:** Imagine you have a rubber band on a basketball vs. on a donut. On the basketball, you can always shrink it to a point. On the donut, some loops can't shrink (the ones going through the hole). Homology counts these "unshrinkable" loops at each dimension.
