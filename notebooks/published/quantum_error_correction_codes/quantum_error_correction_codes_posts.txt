Title: I simulated quantum error correction codes in Python - here's what I learned about protecting quantum information

Body:

**The Problem**

Classical computers handle errors easily - just copy your bits and take a majority vote. But quantum mechanics has the "no-cloning theorem" that forbids copying quantum states. So how do quantum computers deal with errors?

**The Solution: Quantum Error Correction**

Instead of copying, we *encode* one logical qubit into multiple physical qubits using entanglement.

For the 3-qubit bit-flip code:
- |0⟩ becomes |000⟩
- |1⟩ becomes |111⟩
- A superposition α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ becomes α|000⟩ + β|111⟩

**The Clever Part: Syndrome Measurement**

We don't measure the qubits directly (that would destroy the superposition). Instead, we measure "stabilizers" - operators like Z₁Z₂ that tell us if adjacent qubits AGREE, without revealing what they actually are.

The syndrome (s₁, s₂) uniquely identifies which qubit flipped:
- (0,0) = no error
- (1,0) = qubit 1 flipped
- (1,1) = qubit 2 flipped
- (0,1) = qubit 3 flipped

**Results from My Simulation**

I ran Monte Carlo simulations comparing coded vs uncoded qubits:

- Uncoded error rate: p (linear)
- Coded error rate: ≈ 3p² (quadratic!)

Below the threshold p < 0.5, the code ALWAYS helps. At p = 0.1, that's a 3x improvement. At p = 0.01, it's 300x better!

**The Shor Code**

The 3-qubit code only handles bit-flips. The 9-qubit Shor code concatenates bit-flip AND phase-flip protection, correcting any single-qubit error. It's a [[9,1,3]] code: 9 physical qubits, 1 logical qubit, distance 3.

**Why This Matters**

The threshold theorem proves that if hardware error rates stay below ~1%, we can do arbitrarily long quantum computations by using error correction. This is the foundation of fault-tolerant quantum computing.
