CIR model mathematics:

Stochastic differential equation:
dr_t = κ(θ - r_t)dt + σ√r_t dW_t

Conditional distribution (exact):
r_{t+Δt} ~ c·χ²_d(λ)

Where:
• c = σ²(1-e^{-κΔt})/(4κ)
• d = 4κθ/σ² (degrees of freedom)
• λ = 4κe^{-κΔt}r_t/[σ²(1-e^{-κΔt})] (non-centrality)

Stationary: Gamma(2κθ/σ², σ²/(2κ))

Bond pricing admits closed form P(t,T) = A(t,T)e^{-B(t,T)r_t}

Applications: derivatives pricing, credit risk, Heston stochastic volatility.

**Title:** Implemented Cox-Ingersoll-Ross Model - Interest Rates That Never Go Negative

**Body:**

The CIR model is a foundational tool in quantitative finance for modeling interest rate dynamics. Unlike simpler models, it guarantees non-negative rates—essential for realistic financial modeling.

**The Problem with Negative Rates**

Early interest rate models like Vasicek allow negative rates, which (until recent unconventional monetary policy) was considered unrealistic. The CIR model fixes this.

**The Stochastic Differential Equation**

dr_t = κ(θ - r_t)dt + σ√r_t dW_t

Parameters:
- κ (kappa): speed of mean reversion (how fast rates return to θ)
- θ (theta): long-term mean rate
- σ (sigma): volatility coefficient
- W_t: Wiener process (Brownian motion)

**Why the Square Root?**

The key innovation is the σ√r_t diffusion term (not σr_t). This ensures:

1. When r_t approaches zero, volatility decreases
2. The drift κθ dominates near zero, pushing rates back up
3. If Feller condition holds (2κθ ≥ σ²), rates never hit zero

**Simulation Methods Compared**

I implemented two approaches:

**1. Euler-Maruyama (Approximate)**
```python
r_{t+Δt} = r_t + κ(θ - r_t)Δt + σ√r_t √Δt Z
```
Fast but can produce negative values—need reflection/absorption scheme.

**2. Exact Simulation (Preferred)**

The conditional distribution is exactly known:
r_{t+Δt} ~ c × χ²_d(λ)

Where χ²_d(λ) is non-central chi-squared with:
- Degrees of freedom: d = 4κθ/σ²
- Non-centrality: λ = (4κe^{-κΔt}r_t) / [σ²(1-e^{-κΔt})]
- Scale: c = σ²(1-e^{-κΔt})/(4κ)

This method has ZERO discretization error!

**Theoretical Properties**

Conditional mean: 𝔼[r_{t+Δt}|r_t] = r_t e^{-κΔt} + θ(1 - e^{-κΔt})

Conditional variance: Var[r_{t+Δt}|r_t] = r_t(σ²/κ)(e^{-κΔt} - e^{-2κΔt}) + (θσ²/2κ)(1-e^{-κΔt})²

As t→∞, stationary distribution is Gamma with:
- Shape: α = 2κθ/σ²
- Scale: β = σ²/(2κ)
- Mean: θ
- Variance: θσ²/(2κ)

**Implementation Results**

Parameters: κ=0.5, θ=0.05 (5%), σ=0.1, r₀=0.03 (3%)
Feller check: 2×0.5×0.05 = 0.05 ≥ 0.01 = σ² ✓

Simulated 1000 paths over 10 years:
- Sample mean at t=10: 4.79% (theory: 5.00%)
- Sample std at t=10: 2.10% (theory: 2.24%)
- All paths remained non-negative!

**Bond Pricing**

The CIR model admits closed-form solutions for zero-coupon bonds:
P(t,T) = A(t,T) exp[-B(t,T)r_t]

Where A and B are known functions. This makes derivative pricing tractable.

**Applications**

✓ Fixed-income derivatives (caps, floors, swaptions)
✓ Credit risk modeling (default intensity processes)
✓ Stochastic volatility (Heston model uses CIR for variance)
✓ Real options valuation
✓ Pension liability modeling

**Extensions**

- Multi-factor CIR: Multiple correlated processes
- CIR++: Time-dependent θ(t) for calibration
- Jump-diffusion CIR: Add Poisson jumps for sudden rate changes

The CIR model remains a cornerstone of quantitative finance 40 years after its introduction!
