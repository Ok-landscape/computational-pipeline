TITLE: I animated quantum tunneling by solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation — here's what I learned

BODY:

**The Question**

What happens when a quantum particle hits a wall it doesn't have enough energy to climb over?

Classical physics has a simple answer: it bounces back. If you throw a ball at a wall and it doesn't have enough energy to go over, it reflects. Every time. No exceptions.

Quantum mechanics disagrees.

**The Simulation**

I wrote a Python simulation using the split-operator method (also called split-step Fourier method) to solve:

iℏ(∂Ψ/∂t) = ĤΨ

where Ĥ = −(ℏ²/2m)(∂²/∂x²) + V(x)

The initial condition is a Gaussian wavepacket — think of it as a localized "blob" of probability moving to the right with momentum p₀ = ℏk₀.

The barrier is a rectangular potential with height V₀ greater than the particle's kinetic energy E = ℏ²k₀²/2m.

**What Happens**

The wavepacket hits the barrier and *splits*. Part of it reflects back (as classical physics predicts), but part of it *tunnels through* the barrier and appears on the other side.

In momentum space, you can see this clearly: the initial distribution peaked at +k₀ splits into two peaks at +k₀ (transmitted) and −k₀ (reflected).

**Why This Matters**

Quantum tunneling isn't just a curiosity — it's everywhere:
- Alpha decay in radioactive nuclei
- Electron tunneling in flash memory and SSDs
- Scanning tunneling microscopes (STM)
- Nuclear fusion in stars

**The Math**

The split-operator method works by decomposing the time evolution:

exp(−iĤΔt/ℏ) ≈ exp(−iVΔt/2ℏ) · exp(−iT̂Δt/ℏ) · exp(−iVΔt/2ℏ)

The kinetic operator T̂ is diagonal in momentum space (after FFT), so each step is O(N log N). The method is symplectic, unitary, and time-reversible — normalization is preserved exactly.

I recently completed a computational physics project simulating the time-dependent Schrödinger equation to visualize quantum tunneling — one of the most counterintuitive phenomena in physics.

Technical approach:
• Split-operator (split-step Fourier) method for time evolution
• Gaussian wavepacket initial conditions with defined momentum
• Rectangular potential barrier with V₀ > particle kinetic energy
• O(N log N) complexity per timestep via FFT
• Preserved unitarity and probability normalization

Key results:
The simulation demonstrates wavepacket splitting upon barrier interaction — partial transmission (tunneling) and partial reflection — despite classical mechanics predicting 100% reflection.
